BMI for Children and Teens

Adult BMI categories must not be used to judge a child. Growth changes a child’s body quickly, so BMI is compared with other children of the same age and sex.

Why adult categories do not work

A BMI of 20 might be typical for a 15-year-old and very different for an 8-year-old. Boys and girls also grow on different schedules. For this reason, children’s BMI is interpreted using age- and sex-specific percentiles, and this website’s calculator is only for adults.

How percentiles are used

A child’s BMI is plotted on growth charts, such as those from the CDC or WHO, to show what percentage of same-age, same-sex children have a lower BMI. In the CDC system for ages 2 to 19, the categories are:

  • Underweight: below the 5th percentile
  • Healthy weight: 5th percentile to below the 85th
  • Overweight: 85th to below the 95th percentile
  • Obesity: 95th percentile or above

What parents can do

Ask your paediatrician or family doctor to plot your child’s BMI on a growth chart and to look at the trend over time, since a steady curve matters more than one reading. Focus on family routines: regular meals, active play, enough sleep and limited sugary drinks, instead of putting a child on a diet.

Be careful with the language

Avoid commenting on a child’s weight in front of them. If you are worried, raise it privately with a clinician. Also read the medical disclaimer and the adult BMI guide for how interpretation changes after age 20.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use an online adult BMI calculator for my child?

No. The number it returns would be compared against adult cut-offs, which gives a misleading result.

Who should I ask about my child’s weight?

Your paediatrician or family doctor can plot a growth chart and advise on next steps.
