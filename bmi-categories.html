BMI Categories Explained

Adult BMI is grouped into four standard ranges. Here is what each one means, and just as importantly what it does not mean.

Underweight: below 18.5

Can be linked with insufficient nutrition, digestive or thyroid conditions, eating disorders, or simply a naturally slim build. Persistent unintended weight loss deserves medical attention.

Healthy weight: 18.5–24.9

Associated with the lowest average risk of weight-related disease in large studies. It is not a guarantee of good health, and lifestyle still matters.

Overweight: 25.0–29.9

Average risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure begins to rise. Many people in this range are healthy, especially if they are active and carry muscle.

Obesity: 30.0 and above

Clinicians often subdivide this range into classes 1 (30–34.9), 2 (35–39.9) and 3 (40 and above). Higher classes carry greater average risk and may merit a medical plan.

What the categories do not tell you

They do not distinguish muscle from fat, show where fat is stored, or account for age, ethnicity, fitness or family history. Some health bodies recommend lower action thresholds for people of South Asian and some other Asian backgrounds, because risk can rise at a lower BMI.

Next steps

Compare your height and weight on the BMI chart, get an exact figure with the BMI calculator, and read about healthy weight for practical ways to move toward a range that suits you.

Frequently asked questions

Are these categories used worldwide?

The four-category system is the World Health Organization standard for adults, though some countries use adjusted cut-offs for certain populations.

Can I be healthy with a BMI over 25?

Yes. Fitness, diet quality, waist size, blood pressure and blood tests all matter, and BMI alone cannot capture them.
